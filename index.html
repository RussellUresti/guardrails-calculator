How it's calculated
  • Each simulation draws yearly real returns from lognormal distributions. The same random draws are reused for every search and every scenario, so differences between settings come from the assumptions, not from luck.
  • Historical uses Aswath Damodaran's annual US data, 1928–2025 (S&P 500 with dividends, 10-year Treasuries, 3-month T-bills), deflated by CPI; for Global stocks, the equity leg is blended with real developed ex-US data (see below). Windows are sized to your plan length and overlap across the full available span — clamped to 10–75 years for US stocks, but only 10–20 years for Global stocks, since the ex-US data is much shorter. Pessimistic and optimistic are the worst and best windows for your overall stock/bond/cash mix; consensus averages the mean, volatility and correlation across all windows of that length. Each window's mean and volatility then drive the simulation, so the order of good and bad years is randomized rather than replayed.
  • Projected uses published 10-year (J.P. Morgan: 10–15 year) nominal forecasts for US large caps and US aggregate bonds, converted to real with a 2.4% inflation assumption (Schwab, the same report used for cash). Stocks: Research Affiliates 3.1%, Vanguard 4.5% (midpoint of 4–5%), Verus 5.4%, Schwab 5.9%, J.P. Morgan 6.7%. Bonds: Vanguard 4.3% (midpoint), Research Affiliates 4.7%, Schwab 4.8%, J.P. Morgan 4.8%. Cash: Schwab 3.3%. Pessimistic, consensus and optimistic are the lowest, average and highest. Volatility is set to 16.5% for stocks and 6% for bonds.
  • Global stocks (the default) blend each firm's US and international forecasts at your US weight for Projected scenarios, with international split 75% developed and 25% emerging (J.P. Morgan publishes a global figure directly; Verus is left out, no international figure available). For Historical scenarios, the equity leg instead blends real US returns with real developed-ex-US market returns at that same US weight — Fama/French Developed ex US market factor (Kenneth French Data Library, Dartmouth; MSCI-sourced 1991–2006, Bloomberg 2007–present). That data only reaches back to 1991, which limits how far Global-stocks historical scenarios can see; see the next note.
  • The dotted/dashed lines are a check on sequence-of-returns risk: unlike the Monte Carlo band, they don't reshuffle years. Each one replays the literal chronological real-return sequence for every possible historical start year of your exact plan length, run through the same spending and tax rules, so a crash that actually happened early in a given period stays early. Each cohort shown gets two lines — a dotted taxable line and a dashed retirement line — since before your accounts open (and preferentially even after), spending draws down taxable first, so one combined line would hide which bucket is actually doing the work. Worst/typical/best cohorts are shown up to 75 years (US stocks) or 20 years (Global stocks); beyond that only "typical" (median-ending) is shown, since too few distinct periods remain for an extreme pick to mean much; beyond 98 years (US, 1928–2025) or 35 years (Global, 1991–2025) there's no period long enough to replay at all, and no cohort lines appear. Because the ex-US data starts in 1991, Global-stocks cohorts can never include the Great Depression, WWII, or any other pre-1991 crash — only US stocks scenarios see those. "Developed ex-US" also excludes emerging markets, roughly 10% of global market cap today.
  • A small dot marks where any line's taxable or accessible spending money runs out — the point a simulation or cohort actually fails. Lines don't drop to $0 and stay there otherwise: if taxable/bridge funds run dry while retirement money is still off-limits (or a later failure leaves one bucket at zero), that bucket's line goes to $0 and stops, but the other bucket keeps compounding on its own, since that money didn't actually disappear — it's just not what failed. The percentile band doesn't get a marker, since it's a range across thousands of simulations, not a single path.
  • Click any legend label to show or hide that line — useful once several cohort lines are on screen at once. Hidden lines dim in the legend; the chart's scale doesn't change when you hide something, so the remaining lines stay comparable.
  • The chart's vertical axis is scaled so each doubling in value (e.g. $50k → $100k → $200k) gets the same amount of space, rather than each equal dollar amount. Otherwise a 90th-percentile outcome in the tens of millions would squash the median and taxable lines into a few pixels at the bottom.
  • Success means spending is funded every year through the plan age. Before the access age, only taxable and cash can pay the bills. Withdrawals draw the cash reserve first; each year the reserve is then reset to the same multiple of annual spending you started with (e.g. $100k cash on $50k spending = 2 years), refilled from or trimmed into the invested taxable assets, so it does not grow with the portfolio. Withdrawals are grossed up by the tax rates you enter.
  • Triggers scale all balances together, which approximates a market move. Spending stays constant in real terms inside each simulation, as in the Kitces/Income Lab method. Re-run after any adjustment and at least once a year.
